Pitch File 01 — Problem & Market Deep-Dive

Q1: What exactly is rockfall and why is it one of the most lethal hazards in open-pit mining?
A:
- Rockfall is the sudden detachment and falling of rock boulders from open-pit mine walls.
- Benches are 10-15 meters tall with steep 60-75 degree angles exposed to blasting and rain.
- Unlike enclosed tunnel collapses, open-pit rockfalls directly strike unprotected workers and haul trucks.
- Failure is unpredictable: a slope can appear stable for weeks, then collapse in minutes.

Q2: What happened at Kusmunda mine that makes it the ideal pitch anchor for this system?
A:
- Kusmunda is one of India's largest opencast coal mines in Korba, Chhattisgarh (SECL).
- Its deep multi-bench pit goes over 200m deep, creating complex geological stress.
- It produces a huge share of SECL's 150+ million tonnes of coal, making stoppages very costly.
- Anchoring here proves our system solves a real operational hazard at a real high-throughput mine.

Q3: How dangerous are rockfalls in Indian mines specifically, and what does the data show?
A:
- DGMS data consistently ranks slope instability among the top 3 causes of open-pit mining deaths.
- A single bench collapse damages shovels and draglines worth 10 to 50 crore rupees.
- India's 1-billion-tonne coal target forces mines to dig deeper, increasing slope collapse risks.
- Smaller subcontracted mines have higher fatality rates due to zero slope monitoring budgets.

Q4: What is SSR (Slope Stability Radar) and why is it considered the gold standard?
A:
- Slope Stability Radar (SSR) is a ground radar scanning pit walls with sub-millimeter precision.
- It detects pre-failure creep (accelerating movement) hours before a catastrophic collapse.
- It established the industry standard thresholds: Safe (0-50), Warning (50-120), Evacuation (>120 mm/day).
- It is the gold standard for scanned walls, so our system complements rather than competes with it.

Q5: What is the fundamental cost problem with SSR that creates our market opportunity?
A:
- A single SSR unit costs 2 to 4 crore rupees ($250k-$500k USD) plus 10-15% annual maintenance.
- Full perimeter coverage at a mine like Kusmunda requires 4-6 units, costing 12 to 30 crore rupees.
- Most of India's 200+ opencast mines cannot afford this and rely on walking visual inspections.
- This creates an enormous market opportunity for a low-cost, multi-modal monitoring system.

Q6: How does SSR's line-of-sight constraint limit its operational effectiveness even at well-funded mines?
A:
- SSR requires an unobstructed direct line of sight to the rock face it scans.
- Mine benches, corners, stockpiles, and moving haul trucks create large radar shadow zones.
- Even well-funded mines with radar leave 20% to 40% of their pit unmonitored.
- Dust clouds from blasting and dry weather further degrade radar signal quality.

Q7: Who specifically benefits from a cheaper monitoring alternative and what are the market segments?
A:
- 200+ Indian opencast coal mines under Coal India (CIL) and private commercial miners.
- Global open-pit mines across Asia, Africa, and South America where SSR is cost-prohibitive.
- Large mines needing cheap coverage for blind spots, access ramps, and waste dumps.
- Regulators (DGMS) and insurers needing verified slope safety compliance across mines.

Q8: How does the Fukuzono (1985) Inverse Velocity Method provide the physical foundation for our ML pipeline?
A:
- Geomechanical law: the inverse velocity (1/v) of a failing slope drops linearly toward zero.
- Plotting 1/v against time allows geotechnical engineers to predict the exact time of collapse.
- Our AI learns this physical acceleration curve across displacement, vibration, pressure, and strain.
- This makes our system physics-grounded ML rather than arbitrary curve-fitting on random numbers.

Q9: What are the specific displacement velocity thresholds and where do they come from?
A:
- Safe: 0 to 50 mm/day (stable background settlement).
- Warning: 50 to 120 mm/day (onset of accelerating slope creep requiring active monitoring).
- Evacuation: Greater than 120 mm/day (imminent catastrophic failure requiring immediate evacuation).
- Empirically calibrated from Indonesian open-pit coal failure data, matching Indian Permian geology.

Q10: Why are current solutions — visual inspection, manual prussik measurements, and periodic surveys — inadequate for comprehensive mine safety?
A:
- Walking visual inspections cannot see sub-millimeter movement and leave 8-hour shift gaps.
- Prussik wire extensometers only cover a few point locations across a 200-meter wide bench.
- Total station surveys take days to process, which is too slow for fast 6-hour failures.
- Our system provides continuous automated updates every 2.5 seconds over WebSocket.

Q11: How does the Ministry of Mines' policy direction align with our system's value proposition?
A:
- The Ministry of Mines and DGMS are actively mandating digital safety systems under Smart Mining.
- SIH25071 is an official Ministry of Mines problem statement, proving direct government demand.
- Low-cost compliance enables DGMS to mandate continuous monitoring even for smaller state mines.
- District DMFT and PMKKKY funds (1 lakh crore corpus) provide direct funding pathways.

Q12: What is the cost of inaction — what happens when mines operate without slope monitoring?
A:
- A fatal slope failure causes a 30-day DGMS shutdown, costing 150 to 450 crore rupees in lost coal.
- Destroyed draglines and hydraulic shovels cost 15 to 120 crore rupees per unit.
- Irrecoverable loss of human life, devastating miners' families and triggering heavy penalties.
- Total incident cost often exceeds 500 crore rupees, making monitoring a vital investment.

Q13: How does our system complement rather than replace SSR at well-funded mines?
A:
- We act as a coverage extension layer, not an SSR replacement.
- SSR monitors primary critical faces, while our system covers the remaining 20-40% blind spots.
- It costs 5-10% of purchasing extra radar units, filling corners, ramps, and peripheral benches.
- For mines with zero radar, we provide an affordable first-line automated safety alert system.
