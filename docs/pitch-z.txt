PITCH Z — Quick-Fire Cheat Sheet (Non-Technical Teammate Edition)
===================================================================

34 questions. Every answer is under 100 words. Memorise the bold numbers.


SECTION A — Problem & Why It Matters
-------------------------------------

Q1: What is rockfall and why is it deadly in open-pit mining?
A:
- Rockfall is the sudden detachment of rock boulders from open-pit mine walls
- Mine benches are 10-15 meters tall with steep 60-75 degree angles — gravity does the rest
- A single collapse can bury haul trucks, destroy shovels, and kill workers instantly
- Deeper mines mean higher walls and greater gravitational force on unstable rock faces

Q2: What happened at Kusmunda mine and why is it our reference site?
A:
- Kusmunda is one of India's largest opencast coal mines in Korba, Chhattisgarh, operated by SECL
- It runs a deep multi-bench pit over 200 meters deep
- It produces a huge share of SECL's 150+ million tonnes of coal annually
- Its scale, depth, and active slope instability make it the ideal real-world testbed for our system

Q3: How dangerous are rockfalls in Indian mines?
A:
- DGMS data ranks slope instability among the top 3 causes of open-pit mining deaths in India
- A single bench collapse can damage shovels and draglines worth 10-50 crore rupees
- India's 1-billion-tonne coal target is forcing mines to dig deeper, increasing slope failure risk
- Every metre of added depth multiplies the gravitational load on unstable rock faces

Q4: What is SSR and why is it the gold standard?
A:
- SSR (Slope Stability Radar) is ground-based radar that scans pit walls with sub-millimetre precision
- It established the industry thresholds: Safe (0-50 mm/day), Warning (50-120 mm/day), Evacuation (>120 mm/day)
- It provides continuous, real-time deformation monitoring of scanned surfaces
- It is the gold standard — but only for the walls it can actually see

Q5: What is the cost problem with SSR?
A:
- A single SSR unit costs 2-4 crore rupees ($250k-$500k USD)
- Annual maintenance adds 10-15% of the purchase cost on top
- Full pit perimeter coverage requires 4-6 units, totalling 12-30 crore rupees
- Most Indian mines cannot justify or afford this for every active face

Q6: What is SSR's line-of-sight limitation?
A:
- SSR requires unobstructed direct line of sight to the wall it monitors
- Benches, corners, stockpiles, and haul trucks create permanent shadow zones
- Even with 4-6 units, 20-40% of the pit perimeter remains unmonitored
- These blind spots are often the most geologically unstable sections of the wall

Q7: What is the cost of inaction when mines operate without monitoring?
A:
- A fatal slope failure triggers a mandatory 30-day DGMS shutdown costing 150-450 crore rupees in lost coal production (5-15 crore/day)
- Destroyed heavy machinery (draglines, hydraulic shovels) runs 15-120 crore rupees per unit
- Total incident losses frequently exceed 500 crore rupees per event
- Beyond economics, every incident risks irreplaceable worker lives — a cost no balance sheet captures

Q8: Who benefits from our system?
A:
- 200+ Indian opencast coal mines under Coal India (CIL) and private miners need affordable slope monitoring
- Global mines in Asia, Africa, and South America face identical cost and coverage gaps
- Regulators like DGMS gain a digital oversight layer for enforcement
- Insurers get quantified risk data to price policies accurately


SECTION B — What We Built
--------------------------

Q9: Walk me through the system end-to-end. How does data flow from sensors to the dashboard?
A:
- Four data streams feed in: geotechnical sensors, Sentinel-1 SAR satellite radar, Copernicus elevation data, and Open-Meteo rainfall
- FastAPI backend (Python 3.12) formats every stream through Pydantic schemas and builds a 10-feature vector per zone
- ML engine consumes the vector and outputs a RiskPrediction: Safe, Warning, or Evacuation
- Results stream over a persistent WebSocket connection to the Next.js frontend in under 2.5 seconds

Q10: What are the 10 features your model uses?
A:
- displacement_mm_day — daily slope movement speed
- vibration — microseismic tremor intensity
- pore_pressure — groundwater pressure in kPa
- strain — microstrain deformation reading
- slope — hillside angle in degrees
- aspect — compass direction slope faces
- curvature — concavity or convexity of terrain
- vv_backscatter — radar surface roughness signal
- vh_backscatter — radar structural volume signal
- rainfall_mm — daily precipitation amount

Q11: What is the 4x4 zone grid and why 16 zones?
A:
- The Kusmunda open-pit mine spans 4.8 km — too large to monitor as one unit
- We divide it into a 4x4 grid: 16 zones, each roughly 300m x 300m
- 16 zones balances spatial detail against Sentinel-1's 10-metre pixel resolution
- Dataset: 5,696 data points across 356 days
- Distribution: 10 Low-risk zones (62.5%), 4 Medium (25%), 2 High (12.5%)

Q12: Where does your data actually come from?
A:
- Copernicus GLO-30 DEM via Google Earth Engine — 30-metre resolution elevation model for slope, aspect, and curvature
- Sentinel-1 GRD C-band SAR — descending track, VV and VH polarisations, 30 observation dates for radar backscatter
- Open-Meteo ERA5 reanalysis — 356 daily rainfall records for the Kusmunda region
- Synthetic sensors — physics-based generator for displacement, vibration, pore pressure, and strain

Q13: Why synthetic sensor data — how is this credible?
A:
- Active open-pit mines do not grant real-time IoT feeds to hackathon teams; DEM, Sentinel-1 SAR, and ERA5 rainfall are 100% real
- Sensor simulation uses the Fukuzono (1985) inverse-velocity physics model for authentic landslide acceleration curves
- Inter-sensor correlations match published geotechnical literature: pore pressure r=0.918, strain r=0.968
- Calibrated against 4 external benchmarks: Landslide4Sense, NASA GLC, Dorren et al., and DGMS safety records; model is data-agnostic

Q14: What is the Fukuzono method — explain it like I'm not an engineer?
A:
- When a slope is stable, it moves slowly — millimetres per day
- As it approaches failure, movement accelerates. Think of a car rolling downhill faster and faster
- Fukuzono's trick: instead of tracking speed (v), track the inverse of speed (1/v). As failure nears, 1/v drops in a straight line toward zero
- Where that line hits zero on the time axis = predicted failure date
- Our formula: v = 25.0 / (time-to-failure)^0.25. Peak failure predicted at day 338 (25 July 2026)

Q15: What does the live demo actually look like technically?
A:
- Backend: FastAPI on Render (Python 3.12), serves ML predictions and hosts WebSocket endpoint at /ws/feed
- Frontend: Next.js 16 on Vercel, connects via persistent WebSocket — no polling, no page reloads
- Broadcast cadence: one zone updates every 2.5 seconds. Full cycle through all 16 zones = 40 seconds
- Latency: sensor-to-screen under 2.5 seconds per update
- Zero paid APIs, zero proprietary dependencies — fully open-source stack

Q16: What does the MapLibre dashboard actually show?
A:
- A 3D terrain model of the Kusmunda mine pit rendered with MapLibre GL — 100% open-source, no API keys
- The 4x4 zone grid overlays as GeoJSON polygons colour-coded by risk: green = Safe, amber = Warning, red = Evacuation
- Users can tilt, rotate, and zoom the 3D view with mouse controls
- Each zone displays its current risk label and key sensor readings on hover
- Updates in real-time as new predictions arrive over WebSocket

Q17: How does the alert system decide when to fire?
A:
- Safe-to-Warning fires a "warning" alert. Warning-to-Evacuation fires "evacuation." Safe-to-Evacuation fires "evacuation"
- De-duplication: if a zone stays in the same state, the alert is suppressed — no spam
- Downgrades: Evacuation-to-Warning fires an "advisory" so operators know the situation is improving
- Every AlertEvent carries: zone_id, severity, human-readable message, and ISO timestamp
- This prevents alert fatigue — only meaningful state transitions reach the operator


SECTION C — ML & Model Performance
------------------------------------

Q18: What does our model actually predict?
A:
- Three risk levels based on slope displacement rate (millimetres per day)
- Safe (0-50 mm/day): movement within normal geological creep; no action required
- Warning (50-120 mm/day): accelerated movement; increased monitoring, prepare contingency plans
- Evacuation (>120 mm/day): critical threshold breached; immediate personnel evacuation
- Thresholds derived from an Indonesian open-pit coal mine SSR case study (peer-reviewed)

Q19: Why is accuracy misleading and what if the model predicts "Safe" all the time?
A:
- With 61% safe, 26% warning, and 13% evacuation, a dummy model predicting "Safe" achieves ~61% accuracy but misses 100% of evacuations
- Accuracy masks severe class imbalance; we reject raw accuracy and optimize for minority-class Recall, Precision, and F1
- XGBoost achieved 100% recall (0 missed / 197 events) with 97.04% precision
- Random Forest achieved 98.48% recall (only 3 missed / 197) with 99.49% precision — avoiding false alarms while catching critical events

Q20: How do the three models compare?
A:
- Tested on 1,136 sequences; metrics shown for the Evacuation class:

  Model     | Precision | Recall  | F1     | Missed Evacuations
  ----------|-----------|---------|--------|--------------------
  RF        | 0.9949    | 0.9848  | 0.9898 | 3 / 197
  XGBoost   | 0.9704    | 1.0000  | 0.9850 | 0 / 197
  GRU       | 1.0000    | 0.7208  | 0.8378 | 55 / 197

- RF and XGBoost are tree-based ensemble models; GRU is a recurrent neural network for sequential data

Q21: Why did we choose RF over XGBoost as the production model?
A:
- Best F1 balance: 0.9898 with only 3 missed evacuations out of 197 (1.5% miss rate)
- Superior precision (0.9949 vs XGBoost's 0.9704) — prevents operator alert fatigue caused by XGBoost's ~3% false alarms
- Strongest contextual learning: terrain/SAR SHAP contribution is 17.03% in RF vs only 6.90% in XGBoost
- Fast CPU inference: ~1 ms per prediction — runs effortlessly on low-power edge hardware without GPU

Q22: What is SHAP and what does the 17.03% terrain/SAR contribution prove?
A:
- SHAP breaks down individual predictions into exact percentage contributions per feature
- Slope, aspect, curvature, and SAR backscatter account for 17.03% of prediction magnitude in RF (vs 6.90% in XGBoost)
- This proves the model is learning real terrain physics rather than acting as a simple displacement threshold lookup
- Direct validation of our multi-modal data fusion architecture

Q23: What was the v1 to v2 label fix and what are crossover pairs?
A:
- v1: labels depended solely on displacement, so the model ignored terrain and SAR features entirely
- v2: introduced risk_score = displacement x susceptibility_multiplier (0.70-1.30), factoring slope steepness into risk labels
- Created 75 crossover pairs — cases with identical displacement but different risk levels based on terrain susceptibility
- Result: RF terrain/SAR SHAP contribution jumped from 12.27% to 18.63% on validation, forcing the model to learn geological context

Q24: Why class weighting over SMOTE?
A:
- Sensor channels are physically correlated by construction — synthetic interpolation (SMOTE) risks creating physically impossible sensor states
- Class-weighted loss upweights evacuation samples by ~5.4x during training, keeping every training sample physically grounded
- Forces the loss function to heavily penalise missed evacuations without fabricating synthetic data

Q25: What is a temporal split and why not random?
A:
- Data split chronologically with a hard cutoff at 2026-06-03: Train (4,560 rows) vs Test (1,136 rows)
- Random splitting leaks future temporal curves into training, letting models memorise trajectory shapes
- Temporal split strictly replicates real deployment: predicting future risk from past observations

SECTION F — Judge Defense Questions
-------------------------------------

Q33: "Why not use InSAR for deformation monitoring?"
A:
- We use Sentinel-1 GRD amplitude backscatter, not phase-based interferometry (InSAR)
- InSAR requires precise co-registration, complex atmospheric correction, and suffers severe decorrelation in steep open pits
- GRD backscatter shifts serve as a direct proxy for surface moisture and rock roughness changes
- GRD is terrain-corrected, analysis-ready, and lightweight — one reliable feature among 10 in our fusion pipeline

Q34: "What's the alert lead time before a collapse?"
A:
- Ingestion-to-screen alert latency is under 2.5 seconds over live WebSockets (sub-millisecond locally on edge)
- Catches pre-failure acceleration hours before collapse during the Fukuzono tertiary creep phase
- Literature benchmark: IEEE CNN-LSTM early-warning models target ~30-minute mean lead times
- Edge GPIO triggers sirens and strobes instantly without network latency bottlenecks
